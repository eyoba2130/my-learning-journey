## 2.2 Actors, Roles and Relationships

Three key actors take part in a research project.

| Actor | Role |
|-------|------|
| **2.2.1 Student / Researcher** | Usually a graduate student who carries out the research project under an advisor's guidance |
| **2.2.2 Supervisor / Advisor / Mentor** | Gives guidance, feedback, and support to the student throughout the research |
| **2.2.3 Examiner / Evaluator** | Typically an external reviewer who assesses the quality and validity of the research |

### The relationship

- It is based on **mutual respect, communication, and collaboration**.
- Students rely on advisors for mentorship; advisors give feedback and support so students succeed.
- Evaluators give an **external perspective** and help ensure quality and validity.
- An advisor or mentor can also be a student themselves.
- The evaluator's role is a **one-time duty**.
- Connections to keep in mind: **student-advisor** and **student-evaluator**.
- This relationship can greatly affect the success of the project.

### Discussion questions from the slides

1. What are the key roles and responsibilities of each actor?
2. What are their prerequisites?
3. What should they do, and not do, during the entire journey?

## 2.3 The Process

The research process has seven stages:

```
2.3.1 Proposal -> 2.3.2 Problem description -> 2.3.3 Following the objectives
   -> 2.3.4 Presenting and analyzing data -> 2.3.5 Conclusion and future work
   -> 2.3.6 Oral defense -> 2.3.7 Final documentation (thesis)
```

### 2.3.1 Developing a Research Proposal

The proposal lays the foundation for the final research, so think it through carefully.

1. **Choose a topic** that interests you and is worth learning about.
2. **Conduct preliminary research** to confirm viable sources exist.
3. **Formulate a research question** to plan the research.
4. **Review relevant literature** to show what is already known.
5. **Mind-map your literature review** to find patterns, trends, and themes.
6. **Define your research question** and explain how it contributes to your field.
7. **Focus on one step at a time.** Good research takes time, effort, and thought.

**A proposal should include:** Title, Introduction, Aims/Objectives, Literature Review, Research Design and Methodology, Research Questions, Expected Outcomes, Budget and Timeline, Conclusion, Bibliography.

### 2.3.2 Developing the Problem Description

This defines the scope and objectives of the study. It clearly states the research problem, its significance, and the research questions.

- **Define the problem:** the specific issue or gap in knowledge.
- **Establish significance:** why it matters and how it adds to existing knowledge.
- **Formulate research questions:** specific, measurable, and relevant.
- **Provide context and background** so readers understand the problem.
- **Justify the research:** why it is worth doing.

### 2.3.3 Following the Objectives

Keep the work tied to the aim and objectives set in the proposal. (This slide has no further content in the source.)

### 2.3.4 Presenting and Analyzing the Data

- **Data analysis:** collecting, modeling, and analyzing data with statistical and logical methods.
- **Data presentation:** comparing data sets with visual aids. Three forms: **textual, tabular, graphical**.

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| Asking | Start with a clear research question |
| Collecting | Gather information from reliable sources |
| Processing | Edit, code, classify, and tabulate |
| Analyzing | Find patterns or trends, compute measures |
| Interpreting | Make sense of insights and draw conclusions |
| Organizing | Structure the findings |
| Presenting | Communicate clearly and concisely |
| Sharing | Choose a format: report, presentation, visuals |
| Crediting | Give credit to sources |
| Practicing | Rehearse the presentation |
| Getting feedback | Improve based on it |

### 2.3.5 Drawing Conclusions and Identifying Future Work

- The final step of the scientific method, and the most important part of the research process.
- It summarizes what was learned and should **answer the research question** and support or refute the hypothesis.
- A weak conclusion can invalidate results.

**Consider:**

- Summarizing what was learned
- Assessing the strength of the hypothesis
- Analyzing why results differed from predictions
- Suggesting areas for further study

| Method | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **Induction** | Specific observations to general conclusions |
| **Deduction** | General principles to specific conclusions |

> The conclusion should refer back to the report's aim and objectives.

### 2.3.6 Presenting and Defending Orally

An oral defense is a public presentation of the research to faculty and the community. Its purpose is to let examiners see that the student really did the work.

| Tip | Details |
|-----|---------|
| **Prepare** | Make it understandable to someone who has not read the thesis; cover topic, significance, methodology, results, discussion, conclusion; keep backups in multiple formats and places |
| **Structure** | Usually 20-30 minutes plus committee and audience questions; about 10 slides for 20 minutes |
| **Connect** | Use appropriate but natural language; welcome the audience and explain why you did the thesis |
| **Be clear** | State the problem and why it is worth addressing; cite relevant literature |
| **Focus on contributions** | Spend no more than a third of the time on the problem; focus on your contributions |

### 2.3.7 Preparing the Final Research Documentation (Thesis)

| Section | Content |
|---------|---------|
| Introduction | Background and research objectives |
| Literature review | Existing research and the gaps the study addresses |
| Methodology | Research design, data collection, analysis techniques |
| Results | Findings using tables, graphs, or charts |
| Discussion | Interpretation, implications, comparison with previous research |
| Conclusion | Key findings and their implications |
| Recommendation | Future work not yet addressed |
| References | Bibliography / works cited |

## 2.4 Proposal Preparation

### 2.4.1 Choosing a Subject Area

| Factor | Question to ask |
|--------|-----------------|
| Interest | Does it interest you personally and professionally? |
| Feasibility | Do you have the resources and expertise? |
| Expertise | Do you have the skills and knowledge? |
| Relevance | Is it applicable in the real world? |
| Ethics | Does it meet ethical standards? |
| Data availability | Is there enough data? |
| Scope | Is it too broad or too narrow? |
| Time frame | Is there enough time? |
| Mentor | Can you find a mentor with expertise in the topic? |

Also review the literature, and be open to new or interdisciplinary areas, which can lead to innovative ideas and collaborations.

### 2.4.2 Choosing a Problem within the Subject Area

A problem often comes from a thorough review of the literature that reveals **gaps** in understanding.

1. **Identify a topic area** based on expertise and resources.
2. **Explore problems** in the topic by reviewing research and consulting experts.
3. **Narrow down** to a specific problem that addresses a knowledge gap.
4. **Check its characteristics.** A good problem is:

   - Feasible and fundable
   - Interesting and impactful
   - Novel
   - Ethical
   - Relevant and replicable
   - Manageable
   - Appropriate
   - Publishable
   - Systematic and scientific

### 2.4.3 Quality Assurance of Initial Ideas

Make sure the idea is feasible, well defined, and aligned with the project objectives, through literature review, expert consultation, and refining the question. Ask:

- Is the research question clearly defined and relevant?
- Are the objectives well defined and achievable?
- Have limitations and challenges been identified?
- Is the methodology appropriate and feasible?
- Are the resources and timeline realistic?

### 2.4.4 Write the Research Proposal (Self work)

This is where you do the actual writing, using the structure and checklist below.

### 2.4.5 Sample and Acceptable Proposal Structure

1. Title
2. Abstract
3. Introduction
4. Aims and objectives (the aim is the overall outcome; objectives are specific steps to reach it)
5. Problem statement (1-2 paragraphs)
6. Research methodology
7. Prior research
8. Ethical considerations
9. Analysis of information
10. Limitations and key assumptions
11. Timeline and milestones
12. Budget
13. Appendices
14. References

### 2.4.6 Research Proposal Checklist

- [ ] Title of the research project
- [ ] Background and rationale
- [ ] Research objectives or questions
- [ ] Literature review
- [ ] Research methodology
- [ ] Data collection methods
- [ ] Data analysis plan
- [ ] Timeline
- [ ] Budget and resources needed
- [ ] References

## 2.5 Literature Reviews

A literature review is a **critical recap** of what has already been researched on a topic. A good one does not just summarize; it **analyzes, synthesizes, and critically evaluates** to show the state of knowledge.

**Why do one?**

- To find out what is already known.
- To give readers a critical overview.
- To find what is missing (**gaps**).

### 2.5.1 Importance and Roles

A literature review helps researchers to:

| Benefit | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| Gain familiarity | Learn current knowledge, its boundaries and limitations |
| Understand theories | Put the research question in context |
| Evaluate methods | See which methods worked and which did not |
| Avoid duplication | Find a unique approach |
| Identify gaps | Find what new research can address |
| Resolve conflicts | Make sense of contradictory studies |
| Identify needs | Justify the need for more research |
| Locate research | Place your work in the existing literature |
| Calculate impact | Map the progress of knowledge |

It also helps confirm that you asked a proper research question and chose a proper theoretical framework and/or methodology.

### 2.5.2 Skills and Keys to an Effective Review

| Skill | Ability |
|-------|---------|
| Search | Find and access relevant publications |
| Reading | Read and analyze sources carefully |
| Writing | Write a coherent narrative that synthesizes sources |
| Critical thinking | Judge which works make a noteworthy contribution |

### 2.5.3 Literature Sources

Sources include journals, conference proceedings, books, government reports, theses, and online databases. Use a **variety** of sources and critically evaluate their credibility and relevance.

**Where to search:**

- [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/)
- [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/)
- [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/)
- [Web of Science](https://www.webofscience.com/)
- [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/)

| Primary sources | Secondary sources |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Diaries | Journal articles |
| Audio records | Textbooks |
| Transcripts | Dictionaries and encyclopedias |
| Original manuscripts | Biographies |

### 2.5.4 Literature Review Writing (Self work)

| Step | What to do |
|------|------------|
| **1. Search** | Search for relevant literature (for example, on Google Scholar) |
| **2. Evaluate and select** | Read the abstract and references; check citation counts |
| **3. Identify themes, debates, gaps** | Look for trends, patterns, contradictions, influential studies, and gaps |
| **4. Outline the structure** | Choose an organization (below) |
| **5. Write** | Introduction, main body (summarize and synthesize), conclusions |

**Ways to organize a review:**

- **Chronological:** older to more recent publications
- **Thematic:** around key themes
- **Methodological:** compare the research methods used across studies
- **Theoretical:** discuss opposing theories, models, and definitions

## 2.6 Assessment Criteria

Research papers are assessed on:

- Use of a research methodology appropriate to the task
- Analysis of the sources used to address the problem
- Articulation and progression of the major sections
- Correct attribution of sources through citations

**Other criteria:** purpose and intended audience, authority and credibility, currency and timeliness, objectivity.

### Key quality concepts

| Concept | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| **Bias** | A systematic error at any stage that can lead to inaccurate results |
| **Accuracy and precision** | Needed for reliable, trustworthy results |
| **Data collection** | Provides the raw material for analysis and interpretation |
| **Ethics** | Integrity, honesty, and respect in conducting the work |
| **Abstract** | The first thing evaluators look at; keep it clear, concise, and interesting |

## Quick Summary

- Pick a **current, relevant** area, then find a **gap** through literature review.
- A strong research **problem** is feasible, novel, ethical, manageable, and publishable.
- Follow the stages: proposal, problem, objectives, data, conclusion, defense, thesis.
- Build a good relationship with your advisor and prepare for the evaluator.
- A literature review **analyzes and synthesizes**; it does not only summarize.
- Assess research on methodology, sources, structure, citations, bias, accuracy, and ethics.
